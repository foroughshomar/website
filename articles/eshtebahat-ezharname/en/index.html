Mistakes That Make a Tax Return FragileMistakes That Make a Tax Return Fragile

Last-minute rush, inconsistent reports, and undocumented expenses make the file more vulnerable than anything else.

11 May 2026

A tax return is not the place for storytelling. It is the numerical account of a year of business activity. Nevertheless, certain mistakes recur every period that turn a calm document into a highly contentious one. Recognizing these mistakes is a far cheaper form of prevention than a late defense.

The first mistake is starting in the final week of the deadline. When time is short, figures are entered out of habit rather than from supporting documents. A rounded sales figure or an estimated expense may appear to complete the task, but in reality it leaves room for questions. Begin the work in the preceding weeks, even if the final bank statement has not yet arrived.

The second mistake is inconsistency between reports. The performance return, value-added tax return, seasonal transactions report, and the Taxpayers' System (Samaneh-ye Moadian) must all tell the same story. If sales appear higher in one report and lower in another, the first question is not “which software is faulty”; the real question is where the original figure came from. A simple reconciliation table prepared before submission can eliminate many risks.

The third mistake is recording expenses without supporting documents or with documents unrelated to the business activity. A fictitious invoice or a personal expense recorded under the business name may help at the time of filing but will require explanation during review. It is better to retain complete, genuine, and relevant expenses rather than decorative ones.

The fourth mistake is overlooking payroll. The insurance list, payroll tax, and wage expenses in the books must correspond with one another. Discrepancies among these three sources create questions for both the employer and the performance file.

The fifth mistake is forgetting non-routine events. Sale of assets, rental income, insurance claims, bulk discounts, or sales returns—if not reflected in the return—will later surface in account reconciliations. List these events separately.

The sixth mistake is placing complete trust in software output without reviewing the summary. Software is a tool. A human review of total sales, total purchases, and profit will reveal imported errors more quickly.

The seventh mistake is promising yourself “we’ll fix it later.” An amended return is sometimes possible, but it is never straightforward and does not always produce a cleaner record. Treat the first submission with full seriousness.

Precision in numbers and honesty in advice—these two principles have practical meaning when preparing a tax return: do not record any figure whose source you cannot demonstrate, and do not choose a path that looks good only on paper. A calm file is one that you yourself can explain to a colleague.
